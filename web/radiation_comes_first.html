Radiation Comes First
Sheridan Datathon 2026 · NASA OMNI 2010–2020 × GeneLab OSD-118

Radiation comes first. The storm alert comes late — and often not at all.

Astronauts are harmed by solar radiation (energetic protons), not by geomagnetic storms. Using every radiation storm with proton data in NASA's OMNI record, we show the two are linked but not interchangeable: the radiation arrives about two days before the geomagnetic storm — and most radiation storms bring no strong storm at all. A radiation-first advisory is what a crew actually needs.

Proton flux in OMNI ends 4 Mar 2020 — the radiation analysis covers 2010–2020.

🧬 The biology, kept to what the data shows

Which cells the radiation damages.

NASA GeneLab OSD-118 measured gene expression in human fibroblasts flown in space. It confirms these damage-response genes are active in that cell type (strongest detected probe, log scale). It gives no dose-response and does not identify which samples flew — so we draw no flight effect and set no pathway weights; the genes are context, not a computed index.

◎ Why it matters

A two-day warning no storm index can give.

Because the radiation arrives first, proton flux is both the correct crew hazard signal and an early indicator that a geomagnetic storm may hit Earth two to three days later — useful for grid and satellite operators. Beyond low-Earth orbit (Moon, Mars), radiation is the dominant crew threat and geomagnetic indices no longer apply. The operational takeaway: drive radiation decisions by proton flux and NOAA's radiation scale, not by geomagnetic storm alerts.

Part 2 · The model

READYation: a radiation watch before the storm is declared.

NOAA declares a radiation storm once ≥10 MeV proton flux reaches 10 pfu. READYation forecasts that moment. It is IBM Granite TinyTimeMixer r2, a pretrained time-series foundation model, fine-tuned on the proton record so the crew gets a watch while the flux is still rising.

Model: ibm-granite/granite-timeseries-ttm-r2 · trained 2010–2013 · tested on 2014 – Mar 2020, data it never saw
⚙ How it works

It reads 5.3 days of proton flux and forecasts the next 24 hours.

Input

Last 512 readings of ≥10 MeV proton flux from GOES, every 15 min (5.3 days).

Model

Granite TTM r2 keeps its pretrained core frozen. Only the forecasting head is retrained on 2010–2013, with rare flux rises oversampled.

Output

Flux forecast for the next 24 h. If the forecast peak in the next 6 h crosses the alarm level, READYation raises a watch.

Every model was tuned to the same rule, on alert during 1% of quiet time, and scored on the 14 radiation storms that began in the 2014–2020 test period.

Sept 2014 · slow rise, S2

READYation 5.6 h before the storm, vs 4.8 h from flux alone. Flux climbed for hours, so both had time.

Sept 2017 · fast rise, S3

READYation 3.1 h vs 0.2 h from flux alone. The X8.2 flare behind this storm peaked at 16:06 UTC, after the watch began, so the watch reflects flux still elevated from the week's earlier storms, not a forecast of the flare. Its 24 h forecast did reach 1,000 pfu 6.3 h before red.

Fast storms come from flares magnetically well connected to Earth; their protons arrive within minutes. Warning before those needs flare and CME inputs, not proton flux alone.

◎ What READYation gives a crew

Hours of watch before the radiation storm, on NOAA's scale.

The finding above shows the radiation is the crew hazard and arrives first. READYation moves the warning earlier still: a watch a median of 3.8 hours before NOAA would declare the storm, at a cost of about one extra watch a year. For crews beyond low-Earth orbit, that is time to finish or postpone an EVA and prepare shielding before onboard dose rises.

Limits & honesty
  • Only 9 radiation storms were followed by a strong geomagnetic storm, so the 24% has a wide 95% interval (~12–38%).
  • Proton data covers 2010–2020, a relatively weak solar cycle; the 2024 Gannon superstorm is outside it and shown as NO DATA.
  • >10 MeV flux is NOAA's scale, not a dose. Dose behind shielding depends on higher-energy protons (>100 MeV) and on the spacecraft.
  • READYation was tested on 14 storms from one training run. The 37% cut in false alarms is about 18 alarms over six years and should be confirmed with more seeds and more storms.
  • From proton flux alone, fast-rising storms give under an hour of warning. Earlier warning needs flare X-ray and CME inputs (GOES XRS, NASA DONKI).
  • READYation forecasts flux at geostationary orbit, not dose inside a spacecraft. NASA bases Artemis shelter decisions on onboard dose.
  • READYation counts a new storm after 24 h below 10 pfu (41 onsets in 2010–2020), a different grouping from the 33 radiation storms in Part 1.
  • The gene data shows activity in one cell type (fibroblasts), not damage. Linking flux to cellular damage would require GeneLab experiments that irradiate cells at known doses.
